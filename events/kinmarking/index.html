KINMARKING

KINMARKING is a communal series exploring the convergence of the body, rituals of inheritance, the desire for something to persist and the technology of tattooing.

the inquiry

Why do we want something to persist?

KINMARKING is a project developed to explore how humans decide what is worth preserving, how meaning is carried and stored, and how tattooing can become one method of transmission using symbolism and color and composition to express what’s desired to be communicated and preserved.

Its mode or form is a recurring gathering about memory, lineage, preservation, symbolism, meaning and tattooing. I’m interested in the human impulse to mark and preserve things, especially through icons/imagery.

How does meaning move?

Through stories, objects, rituals, images, and bodies, the project considers how meaning moves between people and across generations. Oral history, lineage, family and personal archives, and rituals of inheritance offer different ways into these questions.

What do we receive, what do we interpret, and what do we choose to pass on? A story, an image, or an everyday practice can open questions about how something becomes meaningful and why that meaning matters to us.

What happens when we preserve or embody it?

KINMARKING explores the human impulse to mark and preserve things, especially through the body and through icons and imagery. Tattooing brings symbolism, color, and composition into the inquiry as ways to express what someone wishes to transmit and preserve.

What can an image hold? What changes as a memory becomes a symbol? What remains uncertain or opens to another interpretation? Conversation and making give these questions a place to be explored together.

one aim

Developing a visual vocabulary

One aim of KINMARKING is to expand the ways memory and meaning can be expressed and preserved through tattooing. Through shared examples, conversation, and guided exploration, participants draw out stories, experiences, inherited practices, and associations that might inform an image.

Together, we lay this material out, navigate its relationships, and explore what becomes significant through that engagement. Color, symbolism, and composition offer ways to develop a visual vocabulary for what participants wish to communicate and preserve.

living histories

Tattooing as transmission.

Tattooing has long participated in how people express belonging, remember relationships, and transmit cultural meaning. Within particular spiritual traditions, marks and the rituals surrounding them connect people with ancestors, protection, belief, and responsibilities to others.

Embodied knowledge + inheritance.

KINMARKING invites renewed attention to these relationships between tattooing, embodied knowledge, and rituals of inheritance.

What do we receive through family stories, gestures, images, and silences? How might a tattoo give form to that inheritance, including family secrets and meanings shared selectively or kept private?

A changing frequency.

A mark can become a transmission whose frequency changes over time, resonating differently as its wearer, relationships, and circumstances change.

Through conversation and making, the project explores what we preserve, what we reveal or withhold, and how meaning continues to move.

the gathering

The body as Legend and Archive.

KINMARKING is a communal series about the body, rituals of inheritance, and the desire for something to persist.

The project takes the form of recurring gatherings about memory, lineage, preservation, and tattooing. Each edition approaches the larger inquiry through a particular subject and through the perspectives of its collaborators and participants.

the intervals

Every two months.

Each session introduces a distinct theme to navigate the larger question: How do we carry, store and navigate meaning? We invite participants to explore personal history, meaning, and internal associations through varying lenses to draft visual representations that may become tattoos. 

the exchange

Thinking with others.

Collaboration is part of how this project deepens. Other thinkers bring their own questions, methods, and experiences, helping shape what each gathering explores.

An opening perspective: oral history.

The first edition begins with oral historians and memory workers. Their perspectives open the inquiry through telling and listening, lineage, family and personal archives, and the ways people remember and interpret what has been passed down.

The series begins here intentionally: drawing out personal material gives participants a starting point for interpretation and visual development. Oral history helps establish the material from which color, symbolism, and composition can emerge.

explore edition 01

Further perspectives.

The next two editions explore color and symbolism / iconography in greater depth. Future conversations could involve memory consolidation, ritual marking, tattoo traditions, or grief, alongside thinkers working in neuroscience, anthropology, history, and other fields.

These are possibilities for the inquiry to develop. Each encounter can introduce another way of thinking about interpretation, meaning, and preservation.

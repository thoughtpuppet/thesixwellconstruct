KINMARKING

KINMARKING is a communal series exploring the convergence of the body, rituals of inheritance, the desire for something to persist and the technology of tattooing.

The Inquiry

Embodied knowledge.

The Body as Symbol and Medium.

The body can act as both a subject of symbolic expression and a means of transmission, making that expression tangible. The body also participates in how knowledge is received, experienced, and expressed. Embodied knowledge includes what is passed between people, within communities, and across generations. However, that material does not always originate from a particular autobiographical experience. Someone might be drawn to an image, gesture, color, or practice through cultural familiarity, collective narratives, shared symbolism, or influences they cannot immediately identify or clearly articulate. These encounters become part of how we perceive, feel, and respond, even when we cannot identify where those influences come from.

In Solehman’s Paranoia & Fostered Trauma, holes in the soles of a figure’s feet represent an absence of sure-footedness. The meaning emerges from the relationship between the void and its location in the body. A physical sensation can also offer a clue to what needs to be expressed. Whether that translation is interpreted as intended also depends on the associations available to the person processing it.
KINMARKING's purpose is to help others recognize and develop their own relationships between images, bodily forms, and ideas. The aim is to discover the associations through which their own visual language takes shape, allowing different interpretations and translations to emerge.

Rituals of inheritance.

Inherited patterns, associations, and attachments.

For this project, inheritance extends beyond what is passed between people to include patterns of relation and rituals of attachment we enter simply by existing. Family stories, habits, images, and ways of relating and being are part of this inquiry, alongside the associations and attachments that form through our participation in and observation of the world. What patterns precede us, become part of us, and continue beyond us? What patterns, associations, and attachments connect us with ancestors, predecessors, and peers through collective consciousness? How do we participate in these shared patterns without having to directly encounter those people? How might our deliberate practices engage with relationships we have not consciously recognized? 

Approaching the process as ritual.

KINMARKING approaches the process of embodying knowledge itself as ritual. Within this project, ritual represents a meaningful and intentional process of becoming: how patterns of relation enter our understanding and shape meaning through associations we inherit, recognize, or create. The inquiry considers how images, objects, and other symbolic forms come to represent those associations. How does one thing come to stand for another? What relationships or meanings remain recognizable even when their form changes? How does something we encounter become an association, an attachment, or a conviction? Most importantly, how can we develop an efficient process for identifying these symbolic correspondences and translating them into tattoos?

The function of "ritual".

Calling this process ritual is also a deliberate choice within the investigation. The word brings cultural and spiritual associations of significance, repetition, ceremony, and reverence into how participants approach it. These associations invite sustained attention to what might otherwise go unnoticed. What becomes available to our understanding when we approach the embodiment of knowledge as ritual and the preservation of memory and meaning as a spiritual process? KINMARKING approaches embodiment through the lens of immanence, recognizing the divine within the neurological and biological processes through which knowledge becomes part of us.

Why do we want something to persist?

As relationships and associations become meaningful, what draws us to preserve them? The desire for something to persist may be what brings someone to tattooing. We may want to remember a person, sustain a relationship, preserve a memory, carry forward an inherited story or cultural practice, or give an attachment a form we can encounter again. What do we hope a mark will hold or allow us to return to?

The experiences that emerge may give this question further dimensions. What did participants hope to immortalize, and what does giving it a form reveal about that desire? How might their relationships with those forms change? A possible sequel could grow from these experiences, returning to the desire for persistence and exploring it in greater depth.

Tattooing as transmission.

Tattooing has long participated in how people express belonging, remember relationships, and transmit cultural meaning. Within particular spiritual traditions, marks and the rituals surrounding them connect people with ancestors, protection, belief, and responsibilities to others.

Engaging one thing through another.

An image, a form, or a bodily location can become a way to engage with a relationship, experience, or quality that has acquired significance. KINMARKING explores how symbolic correspondences participate in our understanding, and how tattooing might give them a form we can encounter again.

How might a tattoo express something of an inheritance, an attachment, or a pattern of relationship? What can remain private or unresolved even when an image is visible? Giving meaning a form can also reveal what that form cannot fully hold.

A changing frequency.

A mark can become a transmission whose frequency changes over time, resonating differently as its wearer, relationships, and circumstances change.

Through conversation and making, the project explores what we preserve, what we reveal or withhold, and how meaning continues to move.

Developing a visual vocabulary

One aim of KINMARKING is to expand the ways memory and meaning can be expressed and preserved through tattooing. Drawing out material, navigating its relationships, and interpreting what becomes significant prepare the ground for visual development. Shared examples and guided exploration help participants consider how color, symbolism, composition, and placement might give those relationships a form.

A visual vocabulary develops through this engagement. A shape may express a relationship; a color may activate an association; the position of an image may participate in its meaning. These possibilities can express something that a name, date, or literal likeness cannot. Developing an image also returns questions to the wider inquiry about how meaning forms, persists, and changes.

Mode, Structure, and Development

The Gatherings.

Every other month, each session introduces a distinct theme through which to explore the larger question: How do we carry, store, process and navigate meaning? We invite participants to explore personal history, meaning, and internal associations through varying lenses, then draft visual representations that may become tattoos.

Modes of material collection.

Through interviews, conversation, and prompts, the series helps participants draw out and collect these associations as material for interpretation. Objects, photographs, and stories offer additional points of entry, but they are not prerequisites. The process can begin with an attachment, a response, or a recurring image. Together, participants lay this material out and navigate its relationships. What emerges gives interpretation and visual development something to work with.

Why oral history and archives?

The first edition begins with oral history and archives because recognizing and collecting material gives subsequent exploration a foundation. Oral historians and memory workers bring approaches to asking, listening, contextualizing, and attending to what is expressed or left unspoken. Objects and photographs can support a story, prompt an association, or offer another way into the material.

Thinking with others.

Collaboration is part of how this project deepens. Other thinkers bring their own questions, methods, and experiences, helping shape what each gathering explores.

An opening perspective: oral history.

The first edition is developed in collaboration with Georgia Dusk and its co-founders, Dartricia Rollins and Ashby Combahee, as individual oral historians and memory workers. Their practices bring distinct approaches to asking and listening into the inquiry, with particular attention to context.

Their perspectives help shape the gathering’s questions and the ways participants engage with one another’s material. The format is developing through this exchange between oral history and tattooing.

explore edition 01

Further perspectives.

The next two editions explore color and symbolism and iconography in greater depth. Future conversations could involve memory consolidation, ritual marking, tattoo traditions, or grief, alongside thinkers working in neuroscience, anthropology, history, and other fields.

These are possibilities for the inquiry to develop. Each encounter can introduce another way of thinking about interpretation, meaning, and preservation.
